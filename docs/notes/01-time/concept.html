1Why time is central

Think about the moment a hand touches a cup. In the video, there is a frame where the fingers meet the cup's surface; in the tactile sensor, there is a sample where pressure spikes. Only if we can say these two are the same moment does the data mean "this much force was applied in this scene."

Frames in 30fps video are about 33ms apart; samples in 1kHz tactile data are 1ms apart. If the two streams are off by even a few tens of ms, the contact moment slips by a frame or more, and the labels become wrong.

This is why OpenGraph Labs leads with "sub-millisecond synchronization." Sync sets the floor for data quality.

2How computers keep time

A clock doesn't know what time it is. It only counts ticks.

quartz crystal → oscillator circuit → steady ticks → counter → time = ticks / frequency
  • Crystal oscillator: a circuit that uses the piezoelectric effect of a quartz crystal to oscillate at a very stable frequency. A quartz watch uses 32,768 Hz (215), halved 15 times to get exactly one tick per second.
  • PC: a hardware counter in the CPU/SoC (TSC on x86, the ARM generic timer on Apple Silicon) increments from boot. The OS divides it by its frequency to get elapsed time, and builds its monotonic clock on top of this counter.
  • ESP32: typically runs off a 40MHz crystal, and esp_timer counts microseconds since boot. So its clock restarts at 0 on every power-up.

Where the wall clock comes from

wall clock = real time at boot (offset) + elapsed time since boot (counter)
  • RTC (real-time clock): a separate chip on the motherboard with its own 32,768 Hz crystal and a coin battery. It keeps counting while the PC is off, and the OS reads it at boot for a rough current time.
  • NTP: every few minutes to tens of minutes, it measures the error against a reference server using the same round trip described in section 6. It corrects the offset and also estimates how many ppm the local crystal is off, adjusting the clock's rate. Between syncs, the OS keeps counting on the local crystal.

What "0 ppm" means

ppm is always measured relative to a more accurate reference: run both clocks side by side for a while and compare.

drift (ppm) = (local elapsed − reference elapsed) / reference elapsed × 10^6
  • The ultimate reference is the definition of the SI second: 9,192,631,770 periods of the radiation from a specific transition of the cesium-133 atom. Atomic clocks realize it with errors around 10-15, effectively zero on a ppm scale. TAI and UTC are computed from hundreds of atomic clocks worldwide.
  • Atomic clocks are too big, power-hungry, and expensive to put in every device, so their time is passed down a chain: atomic clocks → GPS satellites (which carry atomic clocks) → GPS receivers / NTP stratum 1 servers → stratum 2 servers → your PC. Each level corrects itself against the one above, and network latency adds a little error at every step.

This project doesn't need absolute time; it needs every stream to agree. Pick the host clock as the reference for a session and align everything else to it. Better still, sensors that share one clock (e.g. all FSRs and the IMU sampled by the same ESP32 timer) have zero drift between them. Design to keep the number of clock domains small.

3Clock domains: offset and drift

The webcam, the ESP32, the audio device, and the PC each count time with their own crystal oscillator. The scope governed by a single clock is called a clock domain. Timestamps from different domains can't be compared directly.

The difference between two clocks breaks down into two components.

  • Offset: the difference between the two clocks' readings at the same instant. It exists in the first place because the clocks started counting at different points (the ESP32 clock starts at 0 when powered on; the PC's monotonic clock starts from an arbitrary point), but the offset is the difference itself, not either starting point.
  • Drift: a difference in rate. Every crystal oscillates at a slightly different frequency, so the gap widens over time. It's measured in ppm (parts per million); 40ppm means the clocks diverge by 40µs every second.

Because of drift, the offset is not a constant: it changes over time. Its value at a chosen reference time, such as the start of a session, is called the initial offset (offset₀). With host time x measured from that reference:

offset(x) = offset₀ + drift × x

Offset is a linear function of host time: the intercept is the initial offset and the slope is the drift. An exaggerated example, with a device clock running 10% fast and starting 5 s ahead:

Host timeDevice timeOffset (device − host)
0 s5 s5 s (= offset₀)
10 s16 s6 s
20 s27 s7 s
30 s38 s8 s

A single measurement gives only the offset at that instant. Drift shows up only when you compare offsets measured at two different times: (8 − 5) / (30 − 0) = 0.1.

How large the drift error gets

DriftAfter 1 minAfter 10 minAfter 1 hour
10 ppm0.6 ms6 ms36 ms
40 ppm2.4 ms24 ms144 ms
100 ppm6 ms60 ms360 ms

A cheap crystal is typically off its nominal frequency (measured against true time) by tens of ppm, and two such clocks can differ from each other by up to the sum. It also varies with temperature. Even a 10-minute recording can end up nearly a video frame off.

4Wall clock vs monotonic clock

Even within one PC there are several kinds of clocks. Two properties matter most.

  • adjustable: can its value be changed externally (NTP correction, user settings)?
  • monotonic: is it guaranteed never to go backwards?
PythonReference pointUse
time.time_ns()Unix epoch (1970-01-01)Human-readable date/time, logs. Unsuitable for measuring intervals
time.monotonic_ns()ArbitraryTimestamps, interval measurement, timeouts
time.perf_counter_ns()ArbitraryHigh-resolution measurement of short intervals (benchmarks)
time.process_time_ns()ArbitraryCPU time used by the process. Stops while sleeping

The wall clock can jump forward or backward the moment NTP corrects it. If that happens mid-recording, timestamps go backwards or develop gaps. It's the same problem as occasionally getting a negative request duration when you measure it with differences of time.time() in a backend.

Note that the problem is not drift accumulated since 1970. The epoch is just the counting origin, and NTP keeps the wall clock close to true time, so in absolute terms it is more accurate than the monotonic clock. The problem is that it is adjustable. A monotonic clock does drift relative to true time (40ppm is only 0.4ms over a 10-second interval), but it never jumps, and that's what interval measurement needs.

A monotonic clock's value has no meaning on its own. Only the difference between two values from the same clock is meaningful. To compare it with another device's monotonic value, you need to find the offset separately.

The actual reference point, resolution, and implementation vary by OS, by language, and by language version. Check this machine's values with labs/1_clocks.py.

5Sampling and jitter

What a sample is

  • A sensor turns a physical quantity into a continuously varying voltage. For an FSR: a finger presses → resistance drops → the voltage in the circuit changes.
  • A computer can't store a continuous value, only a sequence of numbers. So the ESP32's ADC (analog-to-digital converter) turns the voltage at one instant into a number, e.g. 0–4095. That snapshot is one sample.
  • Sampling runs continuously at a fixed interval, whether or not anything is happening. At 1kHz, a 50ms tap yields about 50 samples and a 2-second grasp about 2,000. We sample continuously because we can't know when contact will happen, and because what we want is the shape of the force over time (onset, rise, hold, release).

Where the period comes from, and why Hz

The interval between samples, the period T, is set by us, not by the sensor. A hardware timer counts crystal ticks and fires an interrupt, e.g. every 40,000 ticks of a 40MHz crystal = every 1ms. Sampling is then a repeating event, and Hz simply means "times per second" for any repeating event, not just vibrations.

sampling rate f = 1 / T        T = 1ms  →  f = 1,000 Hz = 1kHz
SensorOne sample =Rate
FSR (tactile)The pressure voltage at that instant1kHz
IMUAcceleration and angular velocity at that instantHundreds of Hz
WebcamOne image (a frame)30–60Hz (= fps)
MicrophoneThe diaphragm voltage at that instant48kHz

Since the timer counts crystal ticks, crystal drift makes the sampling rate drift by the same ppm.

How fast is fast enough

Sample faster than twice the fastest change you want to capture (the Nyquist criterion). Sample slower and fast changes don't just disappear; they show up as fake slow changes, called aliasing (like wagon wheels appearing to spin backwards on film).

  • Hand motion is roughly up to ~10Hz → 30–60fps video is enough.
  • Contact transients and slip vibrations reach hundreds of Hz → tactile needs ~1kHz.
  • Human hearing tops out around 20kHz → audio uses 44.1–48kHz.

These bands are rough figures. Check what the glove data actually needs in A2.

Jitter

At a 1kHz target, every interval should be exactly 1ms. In practice it varies a little every time. That variation is jitter.

Why it wobbles

  • General-purpose OSes like macOS, Linux, and Windows are not real-time OSes. sleep(0.001) is a request to "wake me up no earlier than 1ms from now"; when you actually wake up is up to the scheduler.
  • Other processes, power management, GC, and interrupt handling can delay it further.

Two ways to write the loop

# relative sleep: "sleep 1ms from now"
while True:
    sample()
    sleep(period)

# absolute deadline: "sleep until start + i×period"
for i in count():
    sleep_until(start + i * period)
    sample()

The two differ in how a late wake-up carries over into the next sample. See how that difference shows up in long-run rate and accumulated error with labs/2_sleep_jitter.py.

It's hard to hold a steady 1kHz in a Python loop on a PC. That's why fast sensors like tactile ones are sampled by a microcontroller (ESP32) using a hardware timer, and the PC only receives and stores the data.

6Aligning two clocks: RTT-based offset estimation

The only way to learn the offset between the PC and the ESP32 is to exchange messages. The principle is the same as NTP.

Assuming uplink and downlink latency are equal, the moment the device stamped t2 corresponds to (t1 + t4) / 2 on the host clock. So

offset ≈ t2 − (t1 + t4) / 2

The error equals how wrong that assumption is: half the difference between uplink and downlink latency. No matter how clever the calculation, this error can't exceed RTT/2. The shorter the round trip, the smaller the error bound.

Reducing error with repeated measurements

  • Mean: average the offset estimates from many pings.
  • Minimum RTT: trust only the ping with the shortest RTT. It's the sample with the smallest error bound.

Which is better depends on how latency is distributed. Check it in labs/3_clock_offset.py.

Drift correction

Because of drift, the offset changes over time. So measure the offset at both the start and end of a recording, and draw a straight line between them.

slope  = (offset_end − offset_start) / (t_end − t_start)   # = drift
offset(t) = offset_start + slope × (t − t_start)

If drift isn't constant (e.g. due to temperature changes), a straight line isn't enough. Then you measure periodically during the recording too, and align piecewise.

7Where to take the timestamp

Even for the same tactile sample, there are several places the time could be stamped.

WhereUpsideProblem
On the device at the moment of sampling (device timestamp)Closest to the actual measurement timeIt's in the device clock domain, so it needs offset/drift correction
On the PC at the moment of arrival (host arrival)It's on the host clock from the start, so it's directly comparableUSB latency, buffering, and scheduling delays all get mixed in. Several samples can also arrive bundled together

The standard approach is to stamp on the device and convert to the host clock. That's why SyncField's timestamp records have device_timestamp_ns and clock_domain fields separate from capture_ns. We'll see how they actually handle this in the next topic (reading the SyncField code).
